Not everyone can eat

Food access
at OSU.

In 2023-24, OSU’s Policy Analysis Lab asked students about food access and interviewed nine students who had been identified as food insecure. Their accounts were specific. Food costs meant smaller or skipped meals. Campus schedules and dining hours didn’t always line up. Some students wanted more control over fresh, nutritious options. One student said the lowest meal plan wasn’t enough to get through the term.

Less buying power at select dining centers

Resident discounts fell from 20% in 2025–26 to 15% in 2026–27. At the same menu price, a $1,000 plan balance covers about $1,176 of food at 15% off, versus $1,250 at 20% off.2

The $1,050 lowest plan spread over an 11-week term provides about $13.64 per day, or 1.64 meals at $8.30 each.2

Those stories aren’t a verdict on any one student. They show where campus systems can do better: affordability, choice, hours, and access.

A safety net with limits

The pantry helps.
It can’t do it alone.

The Basic Needs Center is an important resource, and the people who keep it running deserve support. OSU’s own reports also show why a pantry can’t replace affordable everyday food.

In its FY26 budget request, the Basic Needs Center reported that more than 500 people were receiving groceries through its pantry each week. Before 2020, that number was fewer than 200 students per week. The pantry is part of the Oregon Food Bank network, so it’s open to Corvallis community members as well as OSU students. That generosity matters, and it also means the pantry serves needs beyond campus.4

OSU staff have described working within resource constraints and said there are times when no available resource fully meets someone’s need. The center explains that free food is offered at scheduled weekly times as one part of a plan, and that direct aid isn’t its focus. In other words, the BNC is doing vital work, but it can’t be the whole answer to a campus affordability problem. Dining prices, hours, and meal-plan options still matter.5–7

What you can do

Make the
ask together.

Sign, share your experience, and ask the people with a role in campus housing and food service decisions to act. This is a student call for specific, measurable commitments.

Residence Hall Association (RHA)

RHA represents on-campus residents and can elevate resident priorities. Ask your RHA to publicly support affordable food, bring student experiences to UHDS, and report back on progress. Learn about RHA ↗

UHDS dining directors

University Housing & Dining Services directs dining programs and operations. Speak with dining directors to publish affordable meal options and prices, as well as to reevaluate hours and meal-plan value with students. These are decisionmakers with the ability to change dining policies, and the study doesn’t establish personal intent to cause hunger. Contact UHDS ↗
